# Method

Read at Steps 2 to 7. The plan exists to validate one hypothesis fast, not to cover everything. Priority comes before current state: an hour on current state for the wrong problem is the most expensive mistake in discovery.

## 1. Two jobs the point of view does

It proves you did the work, and it surfaces the priority quickly. It is a hypothesis for the buyer to confirm, correct or reject. A wrong one that gets disqualified fast is a win.

## 2. Build the point of view

Two linked sets, each line grounded in a dated, sourced signal.

| Set | Count | Each line carries |
|---|---|---|
| Company focus areas | 3 | The initiative (named where possible), the signal with source and date, a label, a validating question |
| Team focus areas | 3 | What this role owns or is measured on, why it matters to them operationally or personally, the signal, a label, a validating question |

Team areas must ladder up to company areas. Company priorities justify budget; team priorities motivate the champion. Below the C-suite, people are moved by team wins tied to their own success.

Then **rank**. Name one **lead hypothesis**: the fire they would pay to put out, not the fourth-biggest annoyance. Name one **fallback**. The other four stay in reserve, one line each. Depth on one priority beats breadth across twelve; aim for two or three problems you can clearly solve.

**Labels:** `found` (a dated source shows it), `stated` (the seller or buyer said it), `inferred` (your reading), `not queried`. A hypothesis is always `inferred`. Confidence follows `prospect-research`: high needs two independent signals and one in the account's own words; medium one signal or two third-party; low one weak or ageing signal, or no brain.

**Specificity test.** If a line could be true of any company in the industry, it fails. "Reduce downtime" fails. "Complete the [named] migration without customer disruption" passes. Cut or sharpen.

**Signals.** A signal counts only if it is dated and sourced, maps to a pain in the brain (Section 2 `pains`, `triggers`), and changes the angle or a question. Show at most five. Older than `stale_days` (365 default for company priorities, 90 for hiring or leadership moves) is marked stale and cannot support the lead hypothesis alone.

**Dreaded email.** The message this role does not want from their boss (a VP Sales: "How did you miss the forecast this badly?"; a VP Engineering: "We are down. We lost customer data."). It is a hypothesis about a role, never a claim about the person. Use it to choose what to be relevant about.

## 3. Entry state

Check before choosing a path.

| Entry state | Sign | Path |
|---|---|---|
| Cold or outbound | No inbound note, no prior thread | Full path, opener validates the point of view |
| Inbound | A form, reply or request exists | Read it first; the opener builds on it instead of asking what you already know |
| Solution-aware | They name vendors, requirements or a deadline | Do not drag them through the full backward journey; go to P-04, B-03, Q-01 to Q-05 and make the differentiation questions do the work |
| Existing customer | A customer record exists | Route to `customer-context` |
| Open deal past discovery | An open opportunity with a prior record | Short form (`references/modes.md`) |

## 4. Time-box

Read the length from the calendar; default 30 minutes. The plan says which stages to run and which to leave as "if earned".

| Minutes | Run in full | One question each, if earned |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | 1, 2, 3 (one question each) | 4, 7 |
| 30 | 1 to 4 | 5, 6, 7 |
| 45 | 1 to 7 | none |
| 60 or more | 1 to 7, with deeper current state | none |

The goal of a first call is to earn the right to deeper discovery. Do not extract everything at once.

## 5. The seven stages

For each: the going-in hypothesis (from research, to confirm or correct), the questions (from `questions.md`, matched to seniority), and what good looks like.

| # | Stage | Job | Good looks like | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Point of view and priority | Share the point of view, validate it, confirm the priority is being worked now | A named owner, a budget or a date; the buyer corrects or adds | "We will get to it eventually" |
| 2 | Current state | How they address the priority today, scoped to it, deep not wide | You can map the process end to end | A vague, high-level answer |
| 3 | Blockers | Where the friction is. Match directness to the buyer | A specific obstacle in their words | "Nothing really" |
| 4 | Negative consequences | The "so what": why it matters, who else is hit | A cost in time, money, customers or risk; a second stakeholder named | Consequences with no number or no owner |
| 5 | After state | What better looks like; part uncovering, part educating | A bigger picture than they walked in with | They describe a basic fix |
| 6 | Positive business outcomes | How they will measure that it worked, in their numbers | A metric, a baseline, a target, a sign-off | "Improve experience" with no measure |
| 7 | Requirements and next steps | What must be true, how they evaluate, the next dated step | A mutual next step with a date and a second thread | You "easily meet all requirements" |

**Stage 4 detail.** Current state says what is happening; consequences say why it matters. "Siloed data" becomes "cannot decide fast enough to capture share when the market shifts". Consequences tied to a priority are what get executive attention and budget.

**Stage 5 detail.** Share a relevant customer story before asking for their ideal, so they think bigger. The story comes from brain Section 6, approved only. No approved story: say so in the plan; never write "customers like you have seen".

**Stage 7 detail.** Meeting every stated requirement is a red flag. It means every competitor does too and it is a price war. Requirements skew basic; this is where the differentiation questions go.

## 6. Value exchange

Every question gives something back: a reason ("the reason I ask is..."), a teaching point or a customer story. It lands as a conversation, not an interrogation. Read each question back and ask whether a strong seller would say it on a call. If it sounds like a survey, rewrite it.

## 7. The opening

1. **Opener:** `P-01`, or a variant that builds on the inbound note.
2. **Framing line**, an objective not an agenda: "By the end of this call you will know whether we can help you [outcome they care about] at [the level they work at], and I will know [what you need to understand]." No unapproved number or claim in it.
3. **Validate the point of view:** share the lead hypothesis in one or two lines; ask `P-02`; for a director or above, `P-03`.

## 8. Fast disqualify

Ready before the call. Triggers: the priority is acknowledged but not worked; the account matches an `anti_persona` or Section 2 disqualifier; the buyer owns none of the budget or process and cannot reach someone who does. Use a line from `questions.md` section 10 and name the secondary hypothesis. A fast no is a win: it saves both sides time and builds trust.

## 9. The one that goes sideways

State the most likely way this call fails and the move. Examples: the buyer is solution-aware and impatient (meet them where they are); the contact is an IC who answers narrowly (D-01); the priority is real but belongs to another team (ask who owns it and for the intro).

## 10. Pitfalls to name on the plan

Pull two or three that fit this call, concretely:

- Bias-validating questions ("tell me about your role", "what keeps you up at night") tell the buyer you did not prepare.
- Unearned confidence ("I am confident we are a great fit") before discovery.
- A problem versus THE problem: perfect discovery on a non-priority still loses.
- Width over depth.
- Current state without the "so what".
- Defining without quantifying ("reduce risk" does not survive a CFO).
- Meeting all stated requirements.
- Role misalignment: board-level ROI asked of an IC.
- Forcing a solution-aware buyer backward.
- "That makes sense" when it did not: ask for the restart.

## 11. Role fallback

Use the brain first: Section 2 `persona` and `committee_roles`. With no brain, normalise the title to one family and tailor: CTO, VP Engineering, Platform or Infrastructure, Product, Security, Data or AI, DevOps or SRE, Sales, Marketing, Finance, Operations, IT, HR or People, Business Leader (default). Priorities must reflect what the family owns or operates, not company priorities restated in role language. Widen the lens beyond technical roles for GTM, finance and operations buyers.

## 12. Research without a record

No `prospect-research` record, or one past `valid_until`: run a light pass. At most three searches for the account, one for the role (job posts list the outcomes a role owns and the tools they use), one level up and one level down. Prefer earnings remarks, investor materials, executive interviews, engineering blogs, product announcements, partner pages and hiring pages. Cite and date each. If research turns up little, say so and plan on role priors, labelled `inferred`. Offer `prospect-research` for the full brief; never run it unasked.
