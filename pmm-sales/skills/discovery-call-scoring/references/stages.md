# The seven stages

Read at Step 3. Score each stage from what the buyer gave back. Every line below opens How, What or To what extent, so the "In practice" lines you write pass the lint rule.

**Crosswalk to `discovery-call-prep`** (a plan and its review stay on one scale): prep 1 = stages 1 and 2 here; prep 2 = 3; prep 3 = 4; prep 4 = 5; prep 5 = 6; prep 6 = 7. Prep 7 (requirements and next steps) is graded in the Technique line as the set-up of the next call.

## 1. Point of view

What you grade: did the seller earn the right to run discovery? A point of view is an informed hypothesis about company and team objectives, shown for validation. It proves the homework and surfaces priorities fast.

- **Strong:** asked why the buyer agreed to meet; shared two to three minutes of company and team objectives specific enough that they could not describe any company in the industry, ideally tied to a trigger (funding, merger, launch, earnings); asked for corrections and welcomed them.
- **Weak:** generic ("reduce downtime", "improve efficiency"); company with no team tie or the reverse; pitched without validating.
- **Misses that matter:**
  - Bias-validating openers: "tell me about your role", "what are your priorities", "what keeps you up at night". They confirm the buyer's fear that the seller is unprepared. Flag every one.
  - Confidence before understanding ("I am confident we are a great fit"). It confirms the fear that the seller will push regardless. Confidence comes after discovery, tied to what was learned.
- **Lines to coach with:** "What led you to take this call, and what do you want to be sure we cover?" · "What did I get wrong, what did I miss, and which of these is most urgent?"

## 2. Priorities

What you grade: did the seller find the one problem, not a problem? Every company has fires; they choose which to let burn. A perfect cycle on a non-priority still loses.

- **Strong:** isolated one or two priorities worth solving; confirmed they are actively worked, not parked; validated before digging into current state; went narrow.
- **Weak:** went wide, deep on none; accepted an acknowledged problem as a priority (buyers acknowledge almost anything; acknowledgment is not priority).
- **Misses that matter:** width over depth (many problems leave the buyer overwhelmed with no next call; two or three real ones, with confidence they can be solved, is great discovery); no priority test, so flawless discovery may have run on a problem nobody will fund.
- **Lines to coach with:** "How actively is this being worked this quarter, and what would move it up or down your list?"

## 3. Current state

What you grade: did the seller map how the buyer solves the priority today, scoped to it and deep enough to use?

- **Strong:** went straight to current state for the validated priority; followed up until the process was mapped end to end; matched depth to the buyer's role.
- **Weak:** too broad, a list of irrelevant tasks; surface answers accepted.
- **Misses that matter:**
  - Afraid to ask for clarification: "that makes sense" when it clearly did not. Buyers can tell, and a seller who will not admit confusion will not be trusted with the solution. Look for a moment the seller skated past something complex.
  - Role mismatch: executive metrics put to an individual contributor (they check out), workflow minutiae put to a VP.
- **Lines to coach with:** "What happens next in that process, who else is involved, how often does it run, and what happens when it goes wrong?" · to bridge up from a contributor: "What would your VP say matters most if they were on this call?"

## 4. Blockers

What you grade: did the seller find where the current process breaks?

- **Strong:** probed for friction with directness matched to openness; got specific about where and how it breaks.
- **Weak:** never asked; took the first vague answer; assumed the blocker.
- **Lines to coach with:** guarded buyer: "What part of that process creates the most friction?" · open buyer: "What blockers are you seeing?"

## 5. Negative consequences

What you grade: the "so what". Did the seller move from what is happening to why it matters? An improved after state alone rarely beats the risk of change; consequences balance it. Hunt this stage in every review.

- **Strong:** turned each pain into a consequence ("siloed data" became "cannot decide fast enough to take share when the market moves"); quantified where possible; asked who else is affected (breadth doubles as stakeholder discovery); taught a consequence the buyer had not seen, from a customer story or a won-and-lost pattern.
- **Weak:** recorded current state and called it consequence ("data silos, poor analytics", no "so what"); stopped at the first answer.
- **Misses that matter:**
  - Not extracting the "so what". The tell: if this call's notes would look the same as the notes from every other call, discovery was too shallow. Sellers ask, write the answer and move on; they rarely pause to ask what it costs before advancing.
  - Defining without quantifying ("reduce risk", "increase revenue"): direction with no number, which a finance leader cannot act on. Do not over-correct: on a first call an extracted but unquantified consequence is still a win; numbers deepen across calls.
- **Lines to coach with:** "What does that cost you, and who else is affected?" · "What does that do to [their goal] over the next [period]?"

## 6. After state

What you grade: did the seller help the buyer see a bigger version of the solution, then validate it? Part uncovering, part educating. Left alone, buyers imagine a basic solution and the seller competes on price against the most basic rival.

- **Strong:** shared the art of the possible through a relevant, approved customer story before asking for the ideal state; then validated in three steps (below). That flow confirms the after state beats the current state plus the risk of change.
- **Weak:** asked "what would ideal look like" cold and let the buyer define a basic fix; a dreamy future with no metric and no validation.
- **Not reached yet:** common and fine on a first call.
- **Lines to coach with, in order:** "What would change in your process if you had [after state]?" → "How would you measure whether that is an improvement?" → "How much of a gain in [metric] would you need to make fixing this a priority?" (or the cost of inaction: "What does staying as you are cost over the next [period]?")

## 7. Business outcomes

What you grade: the "so what" of the after state. How will the buyer measure success in terms the business, and its economic buyer, care about? "Improve customer experience" is an after state; "more customer spend as a byproduct of higher satisfaction" is an outcome.

- **Strong:** asked the business-outcome question right after the buyer's positive reaction, to anchor value to that moment; tied the metric to a company priority, not only to what the champion owns; laddered a team metric up toward executive or financial impact.
- **Weak:** an after state with no business metric; a metric only the champion cares about, which loses to other departments' initiatives; the seller's benchmark in place of the buyer's own number (finance leaders discount numbers that are not theirs).
- **Not reached yet:** common on early calls.
- **Lines to coach with:** "How would you measure the success of fixing this?" · "What impact would that have on your key metrics?" · "How does that metric show up in what your executives track?"
